Where routers are, and where the trouble is, checked against real paths. The IP database's impossible answers are left out. "Where the trouble is" no longer blames places for delay or loss that never reach the destination.

**On 1.4.x or 1.5.x?** PingerPlot offers this update by itself (or ⋯ → **Check for updates**): click **Install and restart**. If you're running it as administrator, start it normally first. Otherwise: [`PingerPlot-Setup.exe`](https://github.com/jamesccupps/PingerPlot/releases/download/v1.6.0/PingerPlot-Setup.exe) (installs for your Windows user, no admin prompt) or [`PingerPlot-win64.zip`](https://github.com/jamesccupps/PingerPlot/releases/download/v1.6.0/PingerPlot-win64.zip) (portable). Scoop: `scoop update pingerplot`. On Linux: `pipx reinstall pingerplot`. The downloads are code-signed by James Cupps; `SHA256SUMS.txt` lists every file's hash.

### Where routers are

- **Impossible locations are left out.** The IP database puts a carrier's routers at its registered address, and an anycast address wherever its block was registered: 1.1.1.1 answering in 3 ms "in Brisbane", a public resolver in San Jose, a carrier router in Denver one hop after New York at the same round trip. A reply can't come from farther away than light in fiber travels in that time. So an answer farther from a router placed by its name than the two round trips allow is left out of the hop table, the map, the CSV and "where the trouble is", and **Details** says why. It's never replaced by a guess.
- **Doubtful ones too.** A router that answers as fast as the one beside it, which its name places, but that the database puts more than 500 km away is left out as well. That covers a "Washington" between Boston and Quebec City at 16 ms, or a root server one link past London. Hops inside MPLS tunnels, where every router reports the far end's round trip, are skipped.
- **More router names are read**: whole CLLI codes (`ae1-chcgilcpj91`: Chicago) and the metro codes YMQ, YQB, YTO, DSM and CYS (Montreal, Quebec City, Toronto, Des Moines, Cheyenne).
- **One city, one label.** A router placed by its name read "Boston, US", and one placed by the database read "Boston, United States". Both now read "Boston, US". **Details** says the two disagree only when they're more than 150 km apart, not for New York and New York City.

### Where the trouble is

- **Delay counts only as far as it carries**, as loss already did. A router slow to answer its own pings was credited with delay the hops after it didn't have, and 1.1.1.1 answering in 4 ms got "+15 ms in Boston". The places' delays now add up to the destination's.
- **No loss verdict for a destination that never answers.** The loss came from the last router that did, which was only rate-limiting its replies.
- One metro named two ways ("New York" and "New York City") is one row.

### Network

- **The Network column shows the network that runs the address**, not the customer the block is assigned to: a carrier's routers read as a foundation or a trading firm. **Details** adds "Block assigned to" when that's someone else.
